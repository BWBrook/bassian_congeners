I have done the first-pass novelty scan, and the verdict is favourable, with one important qualification: the project is not novel if sold as “these species differ along wet/dry habitat gradients”, because that broad natural-history pattern is already visible in species accounts and at least one older comparative segregation paper. It is potentially novel, and much stronger, if sold as a comparative eco-evolutionary test of how congeneric bird pairs partition ecosystem-structure gradients across spatial and temporal scales, using unusually deep, standardised camera-trap data as the primary inferential engine.

The closest intellectual precursor I found is Richard Loyn’s 2002 paper, Patterns of ecological segregation among forest and woodland birds in south-eastern Australia. That paper examined ecological segregation among many south-eastern Australian bird species pairs and concluded that habitat, range, stratum and substrate were major segregation mechanisms, especially among congeners. Search results also indicate that Common and Brush Bronzewings were among the pairs considered, with Common Bronzewings described as widespread and Brush Bronzewings as more associated with dense forest. This is the key “novelty threat” because it means the project cannot pretend to discover congeneric segregation in these birds from scratch. But it is also a useful foundation: Loyn’s work appears to be broad, synthetic and largely qualitative/comparative, not a detection-aware, camera-trap-based, dynamic occupancy study of repeated partitioning mechanisms across two unrelated congeneric systems.  
https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/osj/1/1/1_1_7/_article

The species-level natural history also broadly supports, but does not exhaust, your field intuition. Brush Bronzewings are described in public species accounts as favouring dense shrub or scrubby habitats, whereas Common Bronzewings are described by the Australian Museum as occurring in almost every habitat type except the most barren areas and densest rainforests. Grey Currawongs are described as occupying a wide range of habitats from forests and woodlands through to more open or semi-arid settings, while Black Currawongs are treated as Tasmanian/Bass Strait birds using a range of Tasmanian habitats. In other words, the literature already gestures at the broad mesic/dense versus open/drier contrast; the project’s novelty must come from quantifying the gradient, decomposing the scale of overlap, and comparing mechanisms across the Bronzewing and Currawong pairs.
https://www.birdsinbackyards.net/species/Phaps-elegans

The Bassian biogeographic layer also has enough substance to be worth keeping, but probably not enough to dominate the first paper. Tasmania’s avifauna has long been interpreted through repeated Pleistocene connection and isolation across Bass Strait, and recent palaeoenvironmental work on the Bassian Land Bridge shows that the exposed land bridge during the last glacial period was not just a passive corridor: vegetation and climate changed substantially, including grassland expansion at the expense of woodland during the late glacial interval. That matters because the presence of Black Currawongs on Tasmania and Bass Strait islands, but not Victoria, need not imply present-day ecological unsuitability in Victoria; it could reflect historical assembly, extirpation, dispersal filters, or post-inundation isolation.
https://archive.org/download/biostor-259400/biostor-259400.pdf

For the Currawong side, the taxonomy and island structure look worth checking carefully before committing claims. The Tasmanian bird list identifies Black Currawong as a Tasmanian endemic, and bird-in-hand resources recognise island subspecies of Black Currawong on King and Flinders islands. That does not prove a particular historical scenario, but it gives the Bassian extension real interpretive potential. I would treat Black Currawong’s absence from Victoria as a provocative secondary question: “Is apparently suitable environmental space unoccupied on the mainland?” rather than “Why is Black Currawong absent from Victoria?” The latter is likely underdetermined without phylogeography, palaeoecology or historical demographic data. 
https://nre.tas.gov.au/wildlife-management/fauna-of-tasmania/birds/complete-list-of-tasmanian-birds

I also checked the methodological neighbourhood. Multi-species occupancy, two-species co-occurrence models, and camera-trap co-occurrence frameworks are mature literatures, not new territory. Devarajan and colleagues review multi-species occupancy models and their use for community and co-occurrence questions; other recent work has developed camera-trap frameworks for interpreting spatial co-occurrence; and the older MacKenzie-style occupancy literature remains central for imperfect-detection inference. This is good news methodologically but bad news if the paper tries to sell itself as a new modelling framework. The project should instead use established machinery to answer a sharper ecological question. 
https://nsojournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ecog.04957

Camera traps for birds are also not inherently novel. There are published examples using camera traps to study ground-dwelling birds, opportunistic camera-trap bird detections, and large-scale camera-trap monitoring programmes. Australian camera-trap datasets such as WildCount have even included Brush Bronzewings in occupancy-style analyses. Again, this does not undermine the project, but it means the manuscript should not say “camera traps can monitor birds” as though that were the main contribution. The distinctive feature here is that these four focal species are unusually well suited to passive camera trapping, and the private dataset is deep enough to support seasonal detection/non-detection, local co-occurrence, and temporal state-transition analyses. 
https://www.mjkelly.info/ewExternalFiles/Murphy%20et%20al%202017%20Bird%20and%20cam%20traps%20Madagascar.pdf

The public-data side is feasible but should be framed cautiously. eBird’s Basic Dataset provides raw observations and metadata and is updated monthly; Birdata/eBird collaboration now supports standardised Australian bird survey protocols such as the 2-ha 20-minute survey; and the ALA provides occurrence records by species, region, date and provider. These are valuable for mainland and range-wide context, but they should not displace the camera-trap network as the main inferential source. The strongest design is camera-first, with eBird/Birdata/ALA used to test whether Tasmanian patterns generalise across the SE mainland and Bassian region. 
https://science.ebird.org/en/use-ebird-data/download-ebird-data-products

The broader ecological literature supports the kind of framing we want. Congeneric species often segregate along environmental gradients, habitat structure, elevation or fine-scale habitat axes; recent work on other bird systems has shown that coexistence can be mediated by environmental habitat gradients and within-habitat segregation. There is also comparative work showing landscape-scale niche segregation in sympatric zones among ecologically similar species. These studies give the proposed paper an international conceptual home: it is not a Tasmanian natural-history note, but a case study in how closely related species partition multidimensional ecosystem space, and how similar partitioning patterns can arise in different lineages. 
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10427832

My novelty assessment is therefore as follows. Low novelty if the paper is framed as mapping distributions of Brush versus Common Bronzewings and Black versus Grey Currawongs. Moderate novelty if it is framed as a Tasmanian camera-trap occupancy study of four birds across habitat gradients. High novelty if it is framed as a comparative test of repeated congeneric partitioning across two unrelated avian lineages, explicitly separating environmental filtering, spatial scale of overlap, and temporal stability of coexistence. The dataset then becomes the enabling instrument, not the topic.

The most defensible gap statement would be something like this:

> Closely related species often segregate along environmental gradients, but most studies quantify this at > a single spatial scale, in a single lineage, or using occurrence data that blur detection, abundance and > temporal turnover. We use an unusually large, standardised camera-trap network across Tasmania, augmented > by public occurrence and survey data from south-eastern Australia, to test whether two unrelated 
> congeneric bird pairs show repeated partitioning along a shared ecosystem-structure gradient, and whether > the mechanisms of overlap differ between a granivorous ground-pigeon pair and an omnivorous currawong 
> pair.

That statement avoids claiming that the habitat differences are unknown. Instead, it claims that the scale, dynamics, repeated structure and mechanism have not been resolved.

The most dangerous near-duplicate remains Loyn 2002. It should be treated generously and explicitly, probably in the first few paragraphs of the Introduction: “Broad comparative work has long suggested that habitat and range segregation structure many south-eastern Australian forest and woodland bird pairs, including congeners. What remains unresolved is how such segregation operates across spatial grain and time, whether equivalent gradients structure unrelated congeneric systems, and whether apparent overlap represents true local coexistence or dynamic replacement.” That positions your study as a modern quantitative successor, not as a rediscovery. 
https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/osj/1/1/1_1_7/_article

The second novelty risk is that reviewers may say the project merely confirms species-account descriptions. The answer is to avoid treating “wet versus dry” as the endpoint. The project should decompose that into measurable structural axes: forest type, topographic wetness, understorey density, productivity, elevation, canopy cover, fire history and agricultural proximity. Your hypothesis should be that the birds are not responding simply to climate, but to alternative ecosystem states along a mesic–xeric structural gradient. That gives the study much broader ecological value.

The third risk is overclaiming competition. Current evidence should be phrased in terms of co-occurrence, segregation, replacement and conditional occupancy, not competitive exclusion. If Currawongs show alternating occupancy or resident/transitory dynamics, that may be consistent with interference, territoriality, priority effects or shifting resource states, but the analysis will not by itself prove competition. For Bronzewings, same-camera and even same-frame co-occurrence would make strong competition claims even less plausible. The more interesting comparison may be that the two genera share a structural gradient but differ in coexistence mode: Bronzewings overlap locally; Currawongs form a dynamic dominance mosaic.

The fourth risk is letting Bassian biogeography become too speculative. It is compelling, but the main empirical strength lies in Tasmania’s camera-trap network. The right structure is probably: first establish the Tasmanian pattern with high-quality data; then ask whether the three cross-strait species occupy comparable public-data environmental space on the SE mainland; then use Black Currawong’s endemicity and island occurrence as a carefully bounded interpretive extension. That could become a second paper if it grows too large.

I would therefore green-light the project as genuinely promising, with the following refined centre of gravity:

> *Main claim to test*: two unrelated congeneric bird pairs independently partition Tasmanian landscapes 
> along a shared ecosystem-structure gradient, but the form of overlap differs: Bronzewings show fine-scale > coexistence, whereas Currawongs show stronger local dominance, transience or temporal replacement.

That is a high-tier ecology pitch if the data support it. It speaks to niche partitioning, scale-dependent coexistence, island biogeography and the value of standardised camera-trap networks, without being parochial. The literature does not obviously contain the finished version of this study; it contains the pieces that make the study timely and interpretable.
